Loading System

How to Know When to Add Weight

Every exercise in the program uses double progression. One rule. Works for every lift. No 1RM testing required.

The Double Progression Rule

1

Pick a starting weight you can do for the bottom of your rep range on all sets

If your rep range is 8–12, start with a weight you can hit 8 clean reps on every set. Not 12. Not failure. Controlled. Conservative is correct here — the weight will go up fast in the first few weeks regardless.

2

Work within the rep range — let the reps climb each session

Session to session, push to complete as many reps as possible within your range. If you got 8, 8, 8, 8 last session, try for 9, 9, 8, 8 this session. The reps naturally accumulate as you adapt to the weight.

3

When you hit the top of your range on every set — add weight

If your range is 8–12 and you complete 12, 12, 12, 12 → add weight next session. Not before. Not when you hit 12 on the first set. All sets. Then add weight.

4

Reset to near the bottom of the range with the new weight

After adding weight you'll naturally drop back toward the bottom of your range. That's correct. Now you climb again. This wave — accumulate reps, add weight, accumulate again — is progressive overload executing exactly as designed.

5

If you can't hit the bottom of your range — drop the weight

If you added weight and you can't hit 8 reps on any set, you jumped too fast. Drop back to the previous weight, complete a session there, then make a smaller jump. There is no shame in this — it's the system working.

Example: Bench Press at 185 lbs, rep range 6–10

Session 1: 185 × 6, 6, 6, 6 — Stay at 185

Session 2: 185 × 7, 7, 7, 6 — Stay at 185

Session 3: 185 × 8, 8, 7, 7 — Stay at 185

Session 4: 185 × 9, 9, 9, 9 — Close — stay

Session 5: 185 × 10, 10, 10, 10 — Add 5 lbs → move to 190

Session 6: 190 × 7, 7, 6, 6 — Normal. Climb again from here.

Accessory & Core Progression

All non-primary exercises follow the same double progression rule but with smaller increments. +5 lbs for upper body accessories (rows, pulldowns, incline press). +5–10 lbs for lower body accessories (leg curl, leg extension, single-leg press). +5 lbs or +1 rep for core exercises (cable crunch: add weight; dead bug: extend range of motion; bird dog: hold longer). The finisher progresses in load carried or rounds completed — add weight to the farmer carry handles every 2–3 weeks, or add a fourth round once three rounds feel manageable.

Effort Calibration

How Hard Should Each Set Feel? (RPE Scale)

RPE — Rate of Perceived Exertion on a scale of 1–10 — tells you how many reps you had left in the tank when you stopped. It's how you know if the weight is right without testing your max. Never train to failure on compound movements. Failure under a heavy barbell at high body weight is an injury waiting to happen.

RPE 7 or below on your last set → Too light

You had 3+ reps left when you stopped. Don't wait for double progression — add weight next session without waiting to hit the top of the rep range. The weight is clearly not challenging enough to drive adaptation.

RPE 8 on your last set → Correct

You stopped with 2 reps clearly still in you — you could have done 2 more but chose not to. This is the target for primary compound lifts on working sets. You're working hard enough to force adaptation without gambling on form breakdown.

RPE 9 on your last set → Acceptable for accessories

1 rep left. Acceptable on accessory exercises where the failure risk is low (leg curl machine, cable crunch, lat pulldown). Not the target for primary barbell movements where a missed rep creates a safety problem.

RPE 10 → Failure. Back off.

You could not complete the rep. This should never happen on primary compound lifts. If you reached failure on bench, squat, or deadlift, something went wrong — the jump was too large, form degraded, or you misjudged fatigue. Reduce weight 5–10% and rebuild.

Rest Protocol

Exact Rest Periods by Exercise Type

Rest periods are not flexible suggestions. For compound movements, insufficient rest directly reduces the weight you can move on the next set, which means you're training a fatigued system rather than a fresh one. For accessories, shorter rest drives metabolic stress — which contributes to fat loss. Each category has a specific protocol.

Exercise Type Rest Period Exercises Why
Primary Compound Lifts 2–3 min Bench, Trap Bar DL, OHP (primary), Leg Press, Hip Thrust Full ATP-PCr system restoration is required to move maximum weight. Short rest here means you're lifting 80% of what you could lift — training harder than necessary to produce a weaker stimulus.
Compound Accessories 90 sec Row (accessory), RDL, OHP (accessory sets), Lat Pulldown, Incline Press, Single-Leg Press Enough recovery to perform quality sets, short enough to keep heart rate elevated. The sweet spot between strength and metabolic demand.
Machine Isolation 60 sec Leg Curl, Leg Extension, Hip Abduction, Face Pull, Rear Delt These are small muscle groups or low-load movements. Full recovery is unnecessary and just pads session time. 60 seconds keeps metabolic demand high.
Core 45–60 sec Dead Bug, Pallof Press, Bird Dog, Cable Crunch, Ab Rollout Core work is not limited by the ATP system — it's limited by neural fatigue and technique. 45–60 seconds is enough to reset form without allowing the core to fully disengage.
Conditioning Finisher Full rest Farmer Carries, Sled Push, Battle Ropes These are max-effort conditioning rounds. Full rest between rounds is correct — the point is to push hard each round, not to accumulate fatigue across rounds. 90 seconds to 2 minutes between rounds. Each round is a sprint effort, not a grind.

Deload

Scheduled Recovery — Every 6–8 Weeks

A deload is a full training week at reduced weight. It is not optional and not a sign of weakness. Accumulated systemic fatigue eventually masks the fitness you've built — deloading strips the fatigue away and lets the adaptation surface. Every elite athlete deloads. It is part of the program, not a break from it.

When to deload

  • Every 6–8 weeks on a schedule, regardless of how you feel
  • Any week where motivation to train has dropped sharply for 3+ days
  • Any week where joints feel achy or "grindy" rather than just muscle soreness
  • After a week where you failed reps that were previously easy

How to deload

  • Train all four sessions as normal — same exercises, same structure
  • Reduce all weights to 60–70% of your current working weight
  • Maintain the same rep ranges — reps should feel very easy
  • No finisher this week — end sessions after the core exercise
  • Focus entirely on form and movement quality at light load

What happens after a deload

Return to full working weights the following week. In most cases you will set a personal record within 1–2 sessions after the deload. The fitness built during the preceding 6–8 weeks was always there — the fatigue was hiding it. This is called supercompensation, and it's why the deload is not optional.

Stall Breakers

When a Lift Stops Moving — In Order

A stall is when you cannot add weight or reps after 3 consecutive sessions at the same weight. Before assuming the program is broken, work through this checklist in order. The answer is almost always one of the first two items.

1

Check sleep first

Strength plateaus are caused by inadequate recovery before inadequate training stimulus — almost every time. If you're getting less than 7 hours per night, no programming change will fix the stall. Sleep is where muscle protein synthesis peaks. Fix sleep before touching anything else.

2

Check protein intake

The 180g/day floor exists for a reason. If you've been hitting 120–140g on most days and calling it close enough, this is where the stall is coming from. Your body cannot rebuild muscle tissue it does not have the raw material for. Log actual intake for a week — most people are surprised by how far short they fall.

3

Take a deload week — even if one isn't scheduled

If sleep and protein are both adequate and you're still stalled, accumulated fatigue is the likely culprit. Take a deload week immediately. Return to full weight the following week and try again. This solves 80% of stalls that aren't nutrition or sleep related.

4

Drop weight 10%, rebuild

After a deload, if you return and still cannot progress past the stall point: drop the weight 10% and rebuild using double progression from there. You will reach and surpass your previous best within 3–5 sessions. The brief backward step is faster than grinding at the stall point for another month.

5

Switch the exercise variation

If a specific stall has been resolved with steps 1–4 but keeps returning at the same weight ceiling, the movement may have run its course for now. On Lower A: switch from leg press to hack squat or vice versa. On Upper A: switch from barbell row to chest-supported row. The new stimulus often breaks the pattern while preserving the movement pattern and muscle group.

Non-Negotiables

Five Rules That Override Everything Else

1

Progressive overload on the four primary lifts, always

If the weight isn't going up over time on bench, OHP, trap bar deadlift, and leg press — nothing else in the program produces results. Every other section in this document exists to support this one rule executing correctly. Stalled lifts → run the stall breaker playbook above. Progressing lifts → everything else will follow.

2

The conditioning finisher closes every session

If short on time, cut an accessory exercise — not the finisher. The finisher is the calorie-burn component of each session. Removing it turns a 400-calorie session into a 280-calorie session. Carry weight, push the sled, or battle-rope for 8–10 minutes. Every single session.

3

Walk 8,000–10,000 steps every day, including rest days

At 380 lbs, daily walking at 10,000 steps burns roughly 900–1,200 calories. No resistance training session can match this consistent daily output. The gym builds muscle and drives EPOC. The walking burns fat between sessions. Both are required — neither replaces the other.

4

180g protein per day is the floor, every day

Body recomposition — losing fat while building muscle simultaneously — requires protein at or above this level. Hitting 180g on gym days and 120g on rest days averages to a number that isn't high enough. The protein target applies every day, independent of training. This is where most people's results diverge from their expectations.

5

Deload every 6–8 weeks without negotiation

The deload is scheduled, not reactive. It goes on the calendar the day the program starts. It is not taken because you're weak — it is taken because supercompensation requires it. The post-deload PR is the payoff. People who skip deloads hit walls that take months to recover from. People who take them keep progressing.
